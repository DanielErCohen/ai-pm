The product question

Copying the estimation software estimators already used was easy. Without reliable drawing extraction, was that enough to compete with it? An AI feature needs an eval to back it or to contradict it, so I set the threshold before building it.

What I tried

My Apr '26 industry review puts a competent estimator at 12 to 16 hours at minimum, on a mid-complexity project, reading drawings to build a scope of work. Only the estimator knows how the scope gets built, so the work is slow and hard to hand over.

Parapet covered the pre-bid workflow: project setup, a record of drawings, a subcontractor database with AI scoring, geo-based bid lists, RFQs, quote parsing with gap analysis against the scope, and project rooms. The rebuilt extraction became the only extraction path in production in Aug '26. Its first production run, in Aug '26, reported success with zero rows. The design partner never used extraction.

I built it alone, product and engineering, with Claude Code agents. Each roadmap brief named the cheapest test that would kill the idea. A dated decision log was never rewritten. Extraction quality had a scored eval rubric (a fixed test set with a scoring sheet). In Sep '26 I added a kill line of 95% recall to the redesign.

Evals

What these measurements can tell us

The 7.5 score combined quality criteria. The later 95% line asked how much of an estimator's scope list the pipeline found. Neither measured time saved in a real estimation task.

The verdict also estimated that drawings held only part of an estimator's list. That estimate came from a separate information-coverage analysis. The public account does not establish a common denominator for those figures, so I do not use them as comparable percentages.

I proposed timing an estimator's review after that verdict. I stopped before running it; the amount of time the product could save remains untested.

An eval is a fixed test set with a scoring sheet. Mine compared the AI's draft of an estimator's scope list (the ROD: one row per item, grouped by trade division) with the list a human estimator wrote for the same drawings. I wrote the thresholds down before each build, so a round could say no.

The criteria

The sheet in use from Sep '26 has 14 criteria, each scored from 0 to 10. The first pipeline used an earlier sheet of 13 criteria, and scores from the two sheets do not compare.

  1. Division coverage: every trade section that carries scope gets rows.
  2. Recall of obtainable scope: how many of the estimator's own rows the AI found.
  3. Source traceability: each row names the sheet it came from.
  4. Confidence calibration: a row marked high is right more often than a row marked low.
  5. Ownership: who supplies and installs each item.
  6. Exclusions: items marked as out of scope.
  7. Quantities: counts and areas on each row.
  8. Precision and volume: few extra rows.
  9. Reviewer correction cost, counted double: how much an estimator must change to use the list.
  10. Addenda: later changes applied to the earlier rows.
  11. Schedule tables: door and finish tables read correctly.
  12. Cross-references: pointers to other sheets are right.
  13. Fabrication control: nothing invented, no sheet that does not exist.
  14. Placement: each item filed under the right trade division.

The headline is the mean of the 14, capped at the correction-cost score plus 2. A list that needs heavy correction cannot score well however much it found.

The thresholds

95% recallShare of the estimator's own rows that the AI found, per drawing set. Below it, extraction leaves the product's core. Written into the rebuild in Sep '26.
Above 7.5 of 10The whole-list score had to hold above this across sets. I set the bar in Aug '26, when I chose to rebuild extraction. 7.5 was the best score any earlier run had reached, in Jun '26.

Rounds on the rebuilt pipeline

From Sep '26 the rebuilt pipeline ran on the 14-criteria sheet. Across six rounds in two weeks the mean rose from 4.3 to a plateau between 5.9 and 6.6. From Sep '26 the correction-cost cap held the headline at 4.0, then 3.0.

The verdict in Sep '26

The pipelines I tested did not hold above 7.5 across drawing sets.

A general-purpose vision model reading a handful of PDFs, steered by one client's past lists, produced round means between 5.9 and 6.6 in the evaluated runs. A second model red-teamed the verdict before I filed it.

  • Five months, two rebuilds and three model families left the best valid rebuilt-round mean at 6.6.
  • The last release shipped ten targeted fixes. One moved its score line: addenda, from 3.0 to 8.0, on the one set that has an addendum.
  • Correction cost sat 10 to 70 times above the target. Across 583 rows on three sets, none was correct in every field.
  • Quantities stayed at 2 to 3 out of 10 for four releases, because the pipeline reads quantities from tables and never measures.
  • The drawings hold 21% to 75% of an estimator's list, median about 60%. A perfect reader still leaves a third to a half of the list to write by hand.

Rows that come from printed schedules (doors, finishes, millwork) and addenda might still have worked. That was unproven, and the same round showed those divisions broken.

The whole-list score stopped being the success metric that day. The new test was the estimator's clock: time one estimator reviewing the AI list against starting from a past list, with a kill line of under 50% of review time saved. I stopped Parapet in Sep '26, nine days after the verdict and before any estimator was timed.

Decision timeline

  1. Apr '26

    Started solo, with Claude Code agents. Each roadmap brief named the cheapest kill test, and a dated decision log was never rewritten. Extraction got a scored eval.

  2. 2 May '26 to 7 Jun '26

    Five scoring rounds on the first pipeline. Only one set reached 7.5.

  3. 12 May '26

    A challenge review found that three sprints of prompt-only fixes had given no durable lift on supplier names and revision tags. It chose a deterministic reader beside the model.

  4. 25 Aug '26

    The first pipeline was deleted. The rebuilt pipeline became the only extraction path in production.

  5. 26 Aug '26

    The first production run of the rebuilt pipeline reported success with zero rows. The design partner had not used extraction.

  6. 3 Sep '26

    I wrote the kill line into the redesign before it was built: 95% recall per drawing set.

  7. 8 Sep '26

    The best set reached 91% of the estimator's rows, against the 95% line.

  8. 16 Sep '26

    The last measured run covered 77% of the estimator's rows across three sets, against the 95% line.

  9. 17 Sep '26

    Verdict: the tested pipelines did not hold above 7.5 across drawing sets. The score stops being the success metric. A timed review by an estimator replaces it, with a kill line of under 50% of review time saved.

  10. 26 Sep '26

    Stopped, before the timed review ran. I removed the deployments and backed up the database. The code is kept and the restart steps are written down.

Questions I asked along the way

  • 26 May '26

    Do we name a price or is it "contact sales"?

    Option rejected
    Publishing a Team price on the site, which the product review recommended because a mid-market buyer needs a number to take internally.
    What happened
    The review's list of resolved decisions recorded "pricing tier: not a priority for now", so I deferred the decision.
  • 23 Aug '26

    Does the reconcile pass move the wrong-division rate at all?

    Option rejected
    Adding a set-level reconcile pass with the largest model on top of the first pipeline. The test was one comparison, with and without the pass, and I would drop it if the wrong-division rate fell by less than 20% relative.
    What happened
    The same day I weighed four options: swap the model only, patch five defects, send the whole PDF in one call, or buy an off-the-shelf takeoff tool. I chose a rebuild that reads each page first and assembles rows afterwards. The first pipeline was deleted in Aug '26, so the comparison never ran.
  • 17 Sep '26

    Can the whole score ever hold above 7.5?

    Option rejected
    One more scored round before showing the output to an estimator, which the Sep '26 handover asked for. I judged that an eleventh round scored by a model could not supply the number the product is judged on.
    What happened
    The answer was no. I replaced the score with an estimator's clock as the test, and I stopped the product in Sep '26, before that test ran.

What happened

Stopped in Sep '26. The pilot ran with one design partner, a mid-market general contractor. The first pipeline never held above 7.5 across sets in five scoring rounds, from May '26 to Jun '26. I rebuilt extraction in August and wrote the 95% recall line into the redesign in early September. No measured set reached it. Five days later the best set reached 91%. A last measured run, a week after that, covered 77%. The next day I wrote the verdict and replaced the score with a timed review by an estimator, with a kill line of under 50% of review time saved. I stopped at the end of September, before that review ran. On the hardest drawings the answer sat across several pages. A prompt could not add that context. I had no body of data to train it on. The build needed more than one person could carry.

I removed the deployments and backed up the database. The code is kept. The restart steps are written down.
